Every batch of computer science and IT graduates in Nepal hits the same wall
around the same time: a degree, a handful of college assignments, and a job
market that doesn't seem to care about either. The advice that circulates —
"just build projects," "learn DSA," "apply everywhere" — isn't wrong, but
it's generic enough to apply to any country's job market. Nepal's tech hiring
has its own shape, and understanding that shape saves months of applying into
a void.

This is a practical look at how the Nepali software job market actually
works, what gets a fresher's application actually looked at, and where the
common advice needs local context to be useful.

---

## Understanding the market you're actually applying into

Nepal's software industry is smaller and more concentrated than the generic
"tech job market" advice assumes, and it's worth being clear-eyed about the
shape of it before job-hunting inside it.

- **It's heavily Kathmandu-centric.** Most established software companies,
  from outsourcing firms to product startups, are based in and around
  Kathmandu and Lalitpur. Remote-first roles exist and are growing, but the
  bulk of entry-level hiring still happens through companies with a physical
  office there.
- **Outsourcing and service companies dominate hiring volume.** A large share
  of the industry works on contract or outsourced development for
  international clients — this is where most fresher hiring actually
  happens, more than at the smaller number of Nepal-based product companies
  building their own software.
- **Salaries vary enormously by company type.** Entry-level pay at a small
  local firm and entry-level pay at an established outsourcing company or
  product company can differ by two to three times for the same job title.
  Broadly, fresher roles tend to start somewhere in the NPR 25,000–50,000 a
  month range, with wide variation either side depending on the company,
  your stack, and your English communication skills — treat any specific
  number you read online as a rough anchor, not a promise.
- **Internships are the primary entry funnel**, more so than in markets
  where fresh graduates are hired directly into full-time roles. A large
  share of junior hires convert from a paid or unpaid internship at the same
  company rather than applying cold into an open "junior developer" posting.

None of this is discouraging news — it's context that changes where you
should actually be spending your effort.

## What actually gets you noticed as a fresher

### A portfolio that shows you can build, not just that you attended a bootcamp

A GitHub full of tutorial clones (the same to-do app, the same weather app
everyone builds from the same YouTube video) doesn't differentiate you —
hiring managers see dozens of identical ones. What does stand out:

- One or two projects that solve a problem specific enough that it's
  obviously not copied from a tutorial — something for a local use case,
  a tool you built because you needed it, an integration with something
  like eSewa or Khalti if you're building anything with payments.
- A working deployed link, not just source code — being able to click
  through something live matters more than most freshers assume.
- A README that explains a decision you made, not just installation steps.
  This is a small thing that almost nobody does, which is exactly why it
  stands out.

### English communication, specifically written communication

A large share of Nepal's software jobs — outsourcing work especially —
involve working directly with international clients, in English, often
async over Slack or email rather than in person. Comfortable, clear written
English is a genuine hiring filter here, not a soft nice-to-have. If this is
a weak point, practicing it (writing clear PR descriptions, clear bug
reports, clear documentation) is time as well spent as another algorithm
problem.

### Practical stack depth over broad, shallow exposure

Nepali hiring for junior roles skews more toward "can you actually build
and ship a working feature in our stack" than toward the heavy
algorithm-interview screening common at large global tech companies. That
doesn't mean fundamentals don't matter — it means depth in one stack (say,
React and Node, or React and NestJS) that you can talk about in real detail
is usually worth more at this stage than shallow familiarity with five
different frameworks.

### A referral or a direct connection, where possible

Given how concentrated the industry is, personal networks carry real weight.
This isn't unique to Nepal, but it's more pronounced in a smaller market —
a lot of fresher hiring happens through college seniors, internship
supervisors, or local tech community connections rather than purely through
cold applications on job boards.

## Where to actually look

- **Local job portals** — Merojob, jobs.com.np, and similar sites are where
  most Nepal-based companies post openings; check them regularly rather
  than relying on one-time searches.
- **LinkedIn**, used actively — following companies you're interested in,
  engaging with posts from people who work there, and reaching out directly
  to ask about openings tends to outperform blind applications through the
  "Easy Apply" button alone.
- **University placement cells and internship programs.** Many companies
  run structured internship intakes tied to specific colleges — worth
  actively asking your department about rather than waiting for it to be
  announced.
- **Local tech communities and meetups** — Kathmandu has an active
  developer community scene (language- and framework-specific meetups,
  Google Developer Group events, hackathons). These are where a lot of the
  informal "we're hiring" conversations actually start, well before a
  listing goes up publicly.
- **Remote and freelance platforms** (Upwork, Toptal, and similar) as a
  parallel path, not necessarily a replacement for local employment —
  freelance work, even part-time alongside a local job search, builds a
  portfolio of real client work and often pays meaningfully better per hour
  than local entry-level salaries, though it comes without the stability or
  mentorship of a first employed role.

## What the interview process usually looks like

For most Nepal-based companies hiring freshers, expect some combination of:

1. **A practical take-home task or small coding exercise** in the stack the
   role uses — closer to "build this small feature" than to a pure
   algorithms whiteboard round.
2. **A technical conversation about your own projects** — being able to
   explain *why* you made a decision in your portfolio project, not just
   that it works, matters more here than in a generic resume screen.
3. **A round assessing communication and English fluency**, sometimes
   informally folded into the technical conversation rather than run as a
   separate step, especially for outsourcing-facing roles.
4. **Salary and expectation discussion** where negotiating room for a true
   fresher is usually limited — the leverage point tends to come at the
   first job change, six months to a year in, once you have real production
   experience to point to.

## Trade-offs worth thinking through deliberately

### Local job vs remote/international work from day one

Remote work for international clients, through Upwork or a direct
international employer, often pays significantly more than a local
entry-level role. The trade-off is stability, mentorship, and structure — a
first local job with an actual team and code review process tends to build
foundational skills faster than solo freelance work does, even at lower pay.
Many developers do both in sequence: a first local job for the grounding,
then a move toward remote or international work once they have real
production experience behind them.

### Internship-to-hire vs applying directly for junior roles

An internship, even unpaid or low-paid, at a legitimate company is often a
faster and more reliable path to a real offer than repeatedly applying for
"junior developer" postings that increasingly expect some prior experience.
The trade-off is accepting lower pay for a defined period in exchange for
production experience and, ideally, a direct conversion path.

### Breadth vs depth in your first learning phase

Learning one stack deeply enough to build and explain a real project beats
shallow exposure to many frameworks, for the specific purpose of getting a
first job. Breadth becomes more valuable later, once you have a foundation
to generalize from.

## Common mistakes

1. **A portfolio of unmodified tutorial projects** that a hiring manager
   immediately recognizes, with nothing showing independent problem-solving.
2. **Applying only through job portals**, skipping the local tech community
   and LinkedIn engagement where a lot of informal hiring actually starts.
3. **Neglecting written English communication**, which is a real filter for
   client-facing and outsourcing work specifically.
4. **Expecting international-market salary benchmarks** for a first local
   role, and being discouraged by the gap instead of planning a realistic
   path toward it.
5. **Grinding algorithm problems exclusively**, at the expense of having
   anything real to show and talk about in an interview.
6. **Giving up on internships** because they're unpaid or low-paid, without
   weighing them against the realistic alternative of a longer cold job
   search with no production experience to show for it.

---

## Practical recommendations

- Build one or two projects that solve a real, specific problem — not
  another tutorial clone — and deploy them somewhere clickable.
- Treat written English communication as a skill to practice deliberately,
  not an assumed strength.
- Go deep in one stack before going broad, at least for your first job
  search.
- Use local job portals, but don't rely on them alone — LinkedIn engagement
  and local tech community involvement surface opportunities portals miss.
- Take a legitimate internship seriously as a primary path, not a fallback.
- Set salary expectations against the realistic local range for a first
  role, and think of remote/international work as a path you build toward
  with experience, not necessarily a starting point.

---

## Conclusion

Getting a first software job in Nepal isn't a smaller version of getting one
anywhere else — it runs on its own mechanics: a concentrated, Kathmandu-heavy
market, outsourcing work as the dominant entry point, internships as the
real funnel more than direct junior hiring, and English communication as a
genuine differentiator rather than an afterthought. None of that makes it
harder in some abstract sense. It just means the generic advice — grind
LeetCode, spam applications — needs to be filtered through what actually
moves the needle here: a real project you can talk about in depth, a
network you've actually built, and a realistic read on where the first job
sits in a longer career path rather than the whole destination.
